\section{Related Work}
\subsection{ACT-R and Production Systems}

Traditional cognitive architectures such as ACT-R model reasoning as a sequence of production cycles in which working memory is matched against condition elements and a single production is selected for execution. This design has been highly successful for explaining efficient symbolic reasoning and bounded rationality in human cognition. However, the Match and Select stages are typically treated as implicit and highly optimized operations, so the actual dynamics of conflict resolution and retrieval are often left as implementation-level details rather than as explicit components of the cognitive model.

This makes the reasoning process appear largely serial, discrete, and linear. In contrast, Micro-ACT-R treats selection as an explicit search process over a structured topological potential landscape. Rather than assuming that production matching is an opaque black box, we model candidate states as spatially organized memory elements whose relative energy or attraction determines retrieval and adaptation.

\subsection{Structured Knowledge and Category-Theoretic Models}

A second line of work emphasizes structured representations of knowledge, including semantic graphs, category-theoretic accounts of cognition, and compositional memory architectures. These approaches highlight the importance of relations, transformations, and algebraic consistency in representing conceptual structure. They provide a strong foundation for modeling symbolic organization and relational reasoning, yet they often focus on the static arrangement of concepts rather than on the dynamics of explicit conflict resolution among competing states.

Micro-ACT-R is aligned with this structural perspective in that knowledge is organized through relations and categories. However, our framework extends this view by coupling representational structure with a dynamical potential field. In this sense, memory is not merely a symbolic graph; it is a topological substrate over which reasoning trajectories emerge, compete, and are corrected.

\subsection{Potential Fields, Graph Dynamics, and Quantum-Inspired Architectures}

Recent work in neural-symbolic systems, energy-based models, and graph-based reasoning has explored the use of potential functions, attractor dynamics, and relational structure to model cognition and optimization. These approaches suggest that cognitive processes may be interpreted as trajectories through a state space shaped by local energies and distributed constraints. Similarly, quantum-inspired models have introduced phase-based or probabilistic mechanisms for representing superposition, interference, and non-classical correlation.

Nevertheless, many of these frameworks either remain primarily optimization-oriented, or adopt quantum concepts as analogies rather than as structured cognitive mechanisms. Micro-ACT-R differs from these approaches by integrating three explicit components: a structured topological memory space, a potential-based dynamics for candidate selection, and a phase-coupled mechanism for coordination across distributed memory states. This combination aims to capture both the structural organization of knowledge and the non-linear character of reasoning under uncertainty.

\section{System Architecture}
\subsection{Overview of the Dual-Layer Structure}

Micro-ACT-R is organized as a dual-layer cognitive architecture composed of a control hub and a topological potential field. The control hub governs symbolic coordination, task-level planning, and constraint management, while the topological field represents the active reasoning substrate in which candidate memory states, concept nodes, and retrieval trajectories are embedded. This separation reflects a central design principle of the architecture: symbolic control is not treated as a separate, opaque production engine, but as a high-level coordinator that interacts with a dynamically structured memory landscape.

The topological layer is responsible for the non-linear dynamics of reasoning. Candidate states are not processed as isolated production rules but as spatially organized entities whose relative positions, affinities, and potential energies determine their activation and competition. In this sense, retrieval and conflict resolution emerge from the geometry of the memory field rather than from a purely serial rule-selection mechanism. The architecture therefore combines symbolic control with a distributed, energy-based reasoning process.

\subsection{Sensory Processing and Particle Encapsulation}

At the lowest level, sensory information and internal memory fragments are transformed into particle-like representations that can be embedded into the reasoning field. Each particle encodes a structured unit of information, including semantic content, contextual relevance, and a spatial position in the current memory topology. This representation allows perceptual and mnemonic inputs to be treated uniformly as candidates for matching, reconfiguration, and selection.

The particle abstraction provides a bridge between raw symbolic content and the dynamical field. Rather than directly invoking a production rule, incoming information is first mapped into a candidate state whose similarity to existing memory elements is computed in terms of topological proximity, relational alignment, and potential compatibility. This process allows the system to represent both uncertainty and salience in a continuous cognitive space rather than in a purely discrete symbolic regime.

\subsection{Topological Mapping and Spatial Allocation}

The second stage of the architecture constructs a memory map in which conceptual units are allocated according to their semantic relations, activation patterns, and retrieval history. This map is not a static graph in the conventional sense; it is a dynamic topological structure whose geometry changes as new information is introduced, old associations are reweighted, and the system revises its current reasoning trajectory.

Each memory node occupies a position determined by its semantic relation to neighboring items, while edges encode compatibility, similarity, or historical transition. Through this organization, the reasoning process becomes spatially informed: candidate states that are nearby in the field tend to reinforce one another, whereas incompatible states create local potential barriers. The result is a memory configuration that supports both association and competition, enabling the system to explore alternative interpretations without collapsing prematurely into a single deterministic rule application.

\subsection{Quantization and Phase Synchronization}

Following spatial allocation, candidate states undergo quantization and phase synchronization. Quantization converts continuous or semi-structured memory states into a finite set of representational units that can be compared, stabilized, and propagated through the system. Phase synchronization then aligns distributed memory elements according to shared contextual constraints, producing coordinated activation patterns among conceptually related states.

This phase-based coordination is inspired by the idea that cognitive coherence emerges not only from local attraction but also from distributed alignment across multiple memory elements. In other words, two or more candidate states may become jointly stabilized when their internal phases are compatible, while conflicting states exhibit phase mismatch and are suppressed. This mechanism gives the architecture a means of representing contextual consistency and non-local relation without committing to a purely linear, rule-by-rule execution model.

\subsection{Knowledge Categories and Memory Storage}

The final component of the architecture concerns the relationship between abstract knowledge categories and the concrete memory substrate. In Micro-ACT-R, knowledge categories are not merely labels attached to rules; they are structural organizations that define the expected topology of memory and the constraints under which candidate states may be compared. This allows conceptual classes, semantic relations, and episodic traces to occupy a shared representational space while preserving category-level organization.

Memory storage is therefore dual in nature: it contains explicit symbolic content and implicit geometric organization. Knowledge categories guide retrieval by defining the relevant region of the field, while the actual activation dynamics arise from the interaction among particles, nodes, and phase-coupled states. This duality allows the system to remain grounded in structured symbolic meaning while also supporting the dynamic, non-linear behavior necessary for flexible reasoning and self-correction.

\section{Mathematical Formulation}

\subsection{Categorical Consistency}

Let $\mathcal{K}$ denote the space of knowledge categories and $\mathcal{M}$ the space of memory states. Each concept $k \in \mathcal{K}$ is mapped to a memory representation $m \in \mathcal{M}$ through a structured encoding function
\[
F : \mathcal{K} \rightarrow \mathcal{M}.
\]
The mapping is assumed to preserve conceptual compatibility such that semantically related categories remain close in the memory manifold:
\[
d_{\mathcal{M}}(F(k_i), F(k_j)) \leq \epsilon \; d_{\mathcal{K}}(k_i, k_j),
\]
where $d_{\mathcal{K}}$ and $d_{\mathcal{M}}$ are appropriate distance functions over the category space and memory space, respectively. This condition ensures that structural similarity in the knowledge layer is reflected in the geometry of the memory field.

Let the active reasoning state be represented as a set of candidate memory vectors
\[
X = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}, \quad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d.
\]
Each $x_i$ corresponds to a memory unit, a concept, or a candidate retrieval state. The architecture therefore treats memory not as a flat lookup table, but as a structured topological manifold whose geometry encodes semantic relations.

\subsection{Potential Energy and Relaxation Dynamics}

Reasoning is modeled as an energy minimization process over the candidate state space. We define a total potential energy
\[
E(X) =
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \|x_i - F(k_i)\|^2
+ \sum_{i<j} W_{ij}\,\psi(\|x_i - x_j\|)
+ \lambda \sum_{i=1}^{n} \rho(x_i),
\]
where:
- $\alpha_i$ weights the fidelity of each candidate state to its associated concept,
- $W_{ij}$ represents relational compatibility or historical association,
- $\psi(\cdot)$ is a distance-dependent interaction potential,
- $\rho(\cdot)$ penalizes unstable or inconsistent states,
- $\lambda$ controls the strength of regularization.

The reasoning dynamics follow a relaxation process:
\[
\dot{x}_i = -\eta \frac{\partial E(X)}{\partial x_i},
\]
where $\eta > 0$ is the relaxation rate. In this formulation, inference corresponds to movement through the energy landscape until a local minimum is reached. Candidate states with low energy are favored, while conflicting alternatives are suppressed by energetic barriers.

This makes the Match \& Select process explicit: selection does not occur as an opaque symbolic operation, but as convergence in a structured potential field.

\subsection{Quantum-Inspired Phase Synchronization}

To account for distributed coherence across candidate states, each memory element is assigned a phase variable $\theta_i \in [0, 2\pi)$. The phase-coupled interaction between two candidate states is governed by
\[
\Phi_{ij} = \gamma_{ij}\cos(\theta_i - \theta_j),
\]
where $\gamma_{ij}$ measures the strength of contextual alignment between $x_i$ and $x_j$. The total synchronization energy is then
\[
E_{\text{phase}}(X,\Theta)
=
- \sum_{i<j} \gamma_{ij}\cos(\theta_i - \theta_j).
\]

The phase dynamics are modeled by
\[
\dot{\theta}_i =
\sum_{j \neq i} \gamma_{ij}\sin(\theta_j - \theta_i)
+ \delta_i,
\]
where $\delta_i$ represents local contextual bias. When phases become aligned, conceptually compatible states reinforce one another; when phases diverge, the corresponding candidates are weakened. This introduces a non-local coordination mechanism similar to quantum-inspired coherence without requiring an actual physical quantum computer.

\subsection{Error Feedback and Topological Deformation}

Reasoning is not assumed to be error-free. Let the discrepancy between a predicted and observed state be
\[
e_i = \|x_i - x_i^{\ast}\|,
\]
where $x_i^{\ast}$ denotes the target or expected representation under contextual constraints. The architecture updates the memory topology through a deformation term
\[
\Delta T = \sum_i \beta_i e_i \nabla_{x_i} \mathcal{R}(x_i),
\]
where $\mathcal{R}$ is a correction operator and $\beta_i$ controls the intensity of adaptation. This mechanism allows the system to backtrack, reweight, or locally reshape the memory field when the current representation fails to satisfy consistency conditions.

As a result, the retrieval process is both adaptive and self-correcting: failed trajectories increase the effective energy of inconsistent states, inducing a topological reconfiguration that guides the system toward a more stable interpretation.

\section{Reasoning \& Algorithms}

Micro-ACT-R does not model inference as a single symbolic production firing event. Instead, reasoning is formulated as an explicit search over a structured memory field in which candidate states are iteratively adjusted until they settle into a coherent, low-energy configuration. The central algorithmic idea is therefore to replace the opaque Match \& Select operation of classical ACT-R-like systems with a differentiable, topological relaxation process.

\subsection{Extended Match \& Select Mechanism}

Let the current task context be represented by a set of sensory observations and memory fragments $C = \{c_1, \dots, c_m\}$. Each fragment is first encoded as a particle-like representation and mapped into a candidate state space $X = \{x_1, \dots, x_n\}$, where each $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d$ denotes a candidate memory node or retrieval state. Rather than matching a fixed rule against working memory, Micro-ACT-R constructs a topological field in which candidate states are positioned according to semantic similarity, historical compatibility, and contextual relevance.

The Match step is therefore not a discrete rule lookup but a geometric assignment:
\[
x_i \leftarrow \mathrm{Embed}(c_i, \mathcal{G}),
\]
where $\mathcal{G}$ denotes the current memory graph and its relational structure. Candidate memory states are then evaluated by a composite energy function
\[
E(X,\Theta) = E_{\mathrm{mem}}(X) + E_{\mathrm{rel}}(X) + E_{\mathrm{phase}}(X,\Theta) + E_{\mathrm{reg}}(X),
\]
where $E_{\mathrm{mem}}$ penalizes mismatch with category-conditioned memory targets, $E_{\mathrm{rel}}$ captures relational compatibility among neighboring states, $E_{\mathrm{phase}}$ evaluates phase coherence across candidate elements, and $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$ prevents unstable or over-activated states from dominating the field.

The Select step is implemented as a convergence process:
\[
\dot{x}_i = -\eta \frac{\partial E(X,\Theta)}{\partial x_i},
\qquad
\dot{\theta}_i =
\sum_{j \neq i} \gamma_{ij}\sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) + \delta_i.
\]
The selected state is the candidate that remains stable after the field relaxes to a local minimum. In this sense, selection is not a binary symbolic operation but a dynamical outcome of energy minimization and phase coherence.

\subsection{Loop Stabilization and Potential Convergence}

Inference in Micro-ACT-R proceeds in iterative cycles. At each cycle, the architecture performs the following sequence:

1. Encode the task context and active memory fragments.
2. Construct or update the topological memory graph.
3. Quantize candidate states into representational units.
4. Initialize phase variables for each candidate.
5. Compute the total energy and its gradients.
6. Update the candidate positions and phases until convergence.
7. Extract the dominant stable candidate set or retrieval region.

This loop can be expressed as:
\[
X^{(t+1)} = X^{(t)} - \eta \nabla_X E(X^{(t)},\Theta^{(t)}),
\]
\[
\Theta^{(t+1)} = \Theta^{(t)} + \tau \,\mathcal{S}(\Theta^{(t)},\Gamma),
\]
where $\mathcal{S}$ denotes the phase synchronization operator. The process terminates when the energy decrease falls below a threshold, when the phase alignment stabilizes, or when the candidate set converges to a fixed attractor basin.

This stabilization mechanism is essential for preventing oscillatory reasoning. Without it, topological updates could continuously reconfigure the field without producing a coherent retrieval decision. By requiring convergence of both state geometry and phase alignment, Micro-ACT-R ensures that reasoning settles into interpretable, stable symbolic outcomes.

\subsection{Error Feedback and Topological Reconfiguration}

Inference is not assumed to be perfectly correct. When a candidate state fails to satisfy contextual constraints, the architecture measures the discrepancy
\[
e_i = \|x_i - x_i^{\ast}\|,
\]
where $x_i^{\ast}$ denotes the expected or target representation under task-specific constraints. This error is then propagated through the topology by a deformation term
\[
\Delta T = \sum_i \beta_i e_i \nabla_{x_i} \mathcal{R}(x_i),
\]
which reshapes the local geometry of the memory field and redistributes activation away from inconsistent candidates. In this way, failed retrieval trajectories do not simply terminate; they modify the effective potential landscape so that future reasoning passes through more compatible states.

This feedback loop provides a mechanism for self-correction. If a candidate state becomes inconsistent with new evidence, its energy is elevated, its phase alignment is reduced, and neighboring states are reweighted accordingly. The resulting system can therefore adaptively backtrack, re-prioritize alternatives, and recover from local minima that would derail a purely linear production rule system.

\subsection{Comparison with Classical Production Rule Matching}

Classical ACT-R-like architectures treat Match \& Select as a largely implicit symbolic operation: working memory is compared against production conditions, and a single production is selected according to utility or conflict resolution. The reasoning process is therefore serial, discrete, and typically opaque to the modeler. Micro-ACT-R departs from this formulation in two fundamental ways.

First, selection is explicit: it arises from the dynamics of a continuous energy landscape rather than from an abstract conflict set. Second, the architecture incorporates distributed coherence via phase synchronization, enabling compatible candidate states to reinforce one another while incompatible states are weakened. As a consequence, inference becomes both spatially organized and temporally adaptive.

These differences are not merely representational; they alter the computational character of cognition. Classical production systems focus on rule dispatch under symbolic constraints, whereas Micro-ACT-R focuses on convergence in a structured semantic field. The result is a reasoning architecture that supports non-linear retrieval, contextual adaptation, and topological correction within a single unified process.

Consequently, Micro-ACT-R reframes cognition as a distributed relaxation problem over memory topology rather than as a sequence of discrete production firings.
