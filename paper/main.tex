\section*{Scope of Version 1.0}

This paper presents Ver. 1.0 of the Conscious Cognitive Architecture (CCA), a language-specialized formulation of the broader research framework. It focuses on how multimodal sensory input, experience- and knowledge-based memory representations, and contextual coherence support interpretation, goal-conditioned action selection, and linguistic response generation. Future versions may extend this framework toward language-independent situational awareness (Ver. 2.0) or affective and motivational dynamics (Ver. 3.0). These extensions are outside the scope of the present paper; its claims should therefore be understood as applying to the Ver. 1.0 formulation.

\section{Abstract}

Traditional cognitive architectures, such as ACT-R, are organized around discrete symbolic processing in which working memory is compared against production rules and a single rule is selected for execution. CCA departs from this formulation by treating cognition as a structured, non-linear process in which sensory information is first encoded and stored in a memory field, then organized topologically, and finally resolved through goal-sensitive action selection before being rendered in language.

The central contribution of this paper is therefore to redefine the cognitive cycle as a sensory-to-topological-to-interpretive-resolution-to-action-resolution-to-linguistic process rather than as a discrete Match \& Select procedure alone. In CCA, perceptual evidence first becomes particle-like memory candidates, which are embedded in a topological manifold and quantized into a finite candidate set. Multiple interpretations are temporarily maintained as a possibility-based superposition in the joint space of experience and knowledge, then updated by contextual evidence and resolved through a first computational collapse. A second, distinct superposition is formed over candidate actions and resolved through goal-conditioned evidence and constraints. Language generation is not a third superposed stage; it is a realization of the selected cognitive and action states. The two collapse stages are computational analogies for evidence-conditioned commitment, not claims about literal quantum processes in the brain.

A second important distinction is the role of output generation. In classical architectures, language is often treated as a downstream symbolic product. In CCA, the Linguistic Response Module is integrated into the same cognitive loop as the sensory and memory modules: the generated utterance is re-analyzed through the architecture, compared against the original task objective, and revised when discourse alignment is insufficient.

\section{Introduction}

Traditional cognitive architectures, such as ACT-R, have provided a powerful account of cognition by organizing reasoning as a sequence of production cycles. In such systems, information in working memory is compared against production conditions, and a single rule is selected according to a competition mechanism. This abstraction has been highly influential because it captures the serial, goal-directed nature of symbolic reasoning.

However, this view of cognition is limited in a crucial way. The Match and Select stages are usually treated as implicit, optimized symbolic operations, and their internal dynamics are not themselves modeled as structured inference processes. As a consequence, the architecture does not explicitly represent how perceptual input is organized, how alternative meanings are maintained before resolution, or how action selection and language generation are jointly grounded in the same memory field.

Conscious Cognitive Architecture (CCA) addresses this limitation by redefining cognition as a process that begins with sensory input and proceeds through topological inference before producing a response. The key claim is that multiple candidate interpretations can be maintained after quantization and updated by contextual evidence before a first commitment is made. A subsequent, distinct action candidate field is conditioned on the selected interpretation, active goal, and admissibility constraints, and is resolved through a second commitment. Language is then generated from the stabilized state instead of being treated as an independent symbolic layer.

This shift is critical. In ACT-R-like systems, reasoning is organized around symbolic selection. In CCA, reasoning is organized around the dynamic organization of perceptual evidence in a memory manifold, followed by two sequential resolutions: first of interpretive ambiguity and then of action alternatives. The architecture therefore treats cognition as a distributed process of sensory grounding, topological organization, inference under uncertainty, and recursive response revision.

The central contribution of this paper is therefore to move beyond the conventional ACT-R view of reasoning as symbolic rule execution. We propose a multimodal cognitive architecture in which perceptual evidence is first converted into particle-like memory states, then organized topologically and quantized, then maintained as a possibility-based interpretive field. Contextual evidence updates this field and produces a first computational collapse to a selected interpretation. A second possibility-based field is formed over goal-conditioned action candidates and resolved by a distinct action-level collapse. This two-stage design distinguishes semantic ambiguity from decision making. Crucially, interpretive candidates are formed in the joint space of experiential evidence and abstract knowledge, where episodic traces, contextual priors, and conceptual structure jointly shape the candidate set.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{ACT-R and Production Systems}

Traditional cognitive architectures such as ACT-R model reasoning as a sequence of production cycles in which working memory is compared against condition elements and a single production is selected according to utility and prior activation. These systems have proven effective for modeling human performance in a wide range of cognitive tasks.

However, such systems remain largely serial and rule-based in their conceptual organization. Their strength lies in symbolic control, but their weakness is that they do not explicitly model the perceptual organization of memory, the maintenance of multiple candidate interpretations, or the dynamic role of goal constraints during action selection. CCA differs by making these processes explicit and by distinguishing between interpretation-level and action-level resolution.

\subsection{Structured Knowledge and Category-Theoretic Models}

A second line of work emphasizes structured representations of knowledge, including semantic graphs, category-theoretic accounts of cognition, and compositional memory architectures. These approaches capture the role of relations, abstractions, and structured compatibility between concepts.

CCA is aligned with this structural perspective in that memory is organized through semantic relations, category labels, and contextual compatibility. However, our framework extends this view by coupling symbolic category structure to a topological memory field and by explicitly modeling inference as candidate competition, contextual updating, and two-stage resolution.

\subsection{Potential Fields, Graph Dynamics, and Quantum-Inspired Architectures}

Recent work in neural-symbolic systems, energy-based models, and graph-based reasoning has explored the use of potential functions, attractor dynamics, and relational structure to model cognition and decision making. These frameworks often emphasize optimization over structured state spaces and can be interpreted as forms of distributed inference.

CCA uses possibility-based superpositions as computational analogies for retaining alternatives before commitment. It distinguishes two such stages: an evidence-conditioned collapse over candidate interpretations and a goal-conditioned collapse over candidate actions. The terms ``superposition'' and ``collapse'' do not assert physical quantum states, measurement, or brain entanglement; they specify a formal procedure for representing weighted alternatives, updating their support, and committing to a selected candidate.

\section{System Architecture}
\subsection{Overview of the Dual-Layer Structure}

CCA is organized as a dual-layer architecture composed of a sensory-control hub and a topological reasoning field. The control hub governs symbolic coordination, task-level planning, and category-level supervision, while the reasoning field organizes memory candidates according to relational compatibility, contextual relevance, and auxiliary phase-based coherence.

This distinction is essential. In conventional production architectures, symbolic control decides what rule should fire. In CCA, symbolic structure serves as a scaffold for the reasoning field, while inference unfolds through candidate competition within a dynamically organized memory manifold. The overall loop can be summarized as follows: sensory input is encoded and stored in a hippocampus-like memory field; candidate concepts are retrieved and topologically organized; continuous or semi-structured states are quantized; a possibility-based interpretation field is updated by contextual evidence and resolved by a first collapse; a distinct possibility-based action field is conditioned on the selected interpretation and goal and resolved by a second collapse; the selected state is converted by the Linguistic Response Module into a natural language utterance; and the utterance is audited and revised if its discourse alignment is insufficient.

\subsection{Sensory Processing and Particle Encapsulation}

At the lowest level, sensory information from visual and auditory channels is transformed into particle-like representations that can be embedded into the reasoning field. Each particle encodes a structural summary of the input, including semantic content, contextual relevance, and modality-specific salience.

The particle abstraction provides a bridge between raw sensory input and the dynamical field. Rather than directly invoking a symbolic rule, incoming information is first mapped into a candidate representation that can be compared, related, and reorganized in memory. This stage is not yet a decision process; it is a grounding phase that converts perception into a set of plausible memory candidates.

\subsection{Topological Mapping and Spatial Allocation}

The second stage constructs a memory map in which conceptual units are allocated according to semantic similarity, activation, and retrieval history. Each memory node occupies a position determined by its relationship to other concepts, task context, and prior memory access patterns.

Through this organization, the reasoning field supports a form of context-sensitive navigation in which interpretations are not selected from a flat symbolic inventory but emerge from spatial organization and relational compatibility. This stage produces a structured but still uncertain state space in which multiple semantic hypotheses may coexist under shared contextual constraints.

\subsection{Experience and Knowledge Spaces}

The critical feature of CCA is that candidate states are not formed only from the current memory graph. Instead, the architecture accesses two interacting representational domains: an experience space $\mathcal{E}$ and a knowledge space $\mathcal{K}$. The experience space stores sensory traces, recent episodic fragments, context-sensitive priors, and memory associations derived from prior interaction. The knowledge space stores abstract categories, semantic relations, and symbolic constraints that organize the meaning of the current state.

The candidate state is therefore formed in their joint space,
\[
x_i = g(e_i, k_i), \qquad e_i \in \mathcal{E},\; k_i \in \mathcal{K},
\]
where $e_i$ denotes an experience-based memory fragment and $k_i$ denotes a knowledge-based categorical anchor. This joint construction is essential because the architecture does not assume that interpretation is driven by abstract knowledge alone or by raw sensory input alone. Instead, semantically plausible candidates are generated by the interaction between experiential evidence and conceptual structure.

The candidate field is therefore context-dependent: the support for each interpretation depends jointly on the available experiential trace and the relevant conceptual frame. This dependence is represented through candidate features and contextual compatibility, without requiring a physical or literal quantum entanglement claim.

\subsection{Regulatory Boundaries and Action-Envelope Constraints}

Once candidate states are embedded in the memory manifold, their coordinates do not merely denote semantic similarity. They also define a local action envelope: a region of the space that remains admissible under task constraints, category compatibility, and goal-specific rules. These boundaries are enforced through a regulatory barrier function.

Let $x_i$ denote the coordinate of a candidate state and let $B(x_i)$ be a regulatory barrier function that penalizes motion outside the admissible region. The effective reasoning objective then becomes
\[
E_{\text{total}}(X,\Theta) = E(X,\Theta) + \lambda_B \sum_i B(x_i),
\]
where $\lambda_B$ controls the strength of the boundary constraint. When a candidate moves toward a region associated with incompatible categories, prohibited actions, or legally invalid interpretations, the barrier term strongly penalizes that movement.

This mechanism can be viewed as a computational analogue of a dorsolateral prefrontal-like control function. It is not presented as a literal claim about human frontal cortex anatomy; rather, it captures the idea that deliberation is constrained by admissibility and goal compatibility.

\subsection{Quantization and Contextual Coherence}

Following spatial allocation, candidate states undergo quantization. Quantization converts continuous or semi-structured memory states into finite representational units, which makes comparison and competition tractable over a structured candidate set.

The architecture may also use phase-based coherence as an auxiliary compatibility signal across memory elements. This signal can contribute to candidate scoring or to evidence compatibility at either resolution stage, but it does not itself select an interpretation or action and does not replace the two explicit evidence-conditioned updates described below.

\subsection{Interpretive Superposition and Evidence-Conditioned Collapse}

After candidate states have been quantized, CCA may retain multiple candidate interpretations in a possibility-based superposition until contextual evidence is evaluated. This is the first resolution stage; it concerns what the current evidence means, not what action should be taken. Let $\{|x_i\rangle\}_{i=1}^{n}$ denote the quantized candidate states. Their normalized representation is
\[
|\psi_{\mathrm{int}}\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i |x_i\rangle,
\qquad \sum_{i=1}^{n}|c_i|^2=1,
\]
where $c_i$ is a complex-valued weight encoding the current support for candidate $x_i$. This notation is a computational analogy for a weighted candidate field and does not assert that cognitive states are physical quantum states.

Let $y$ denote newly available evidence and let $C$ denote the active task context. An evidence operator $M_{y,C}$ updates the candidate field. In the candidate basis, a simple diagonal form is
\[
M_{y,C} = \sum_i \sqrt{L(y\mid x_i,C)}\,|x_i\rangle\langle x_i|,
\]
where $L(y\mid x_i,C) \geq 0$ measures the compatibility of the evidence and context with candidate $x_i$. The normalized updated field is
\[
|\psi'_{\mathrm{int}}\rangle =
\frac{M_{y,C}|\psi_{\mathrm{int}}\rangle}
{\sqrt{\langle\psi_{\mathrm{int}}|M_{y,C}^{\dagger}M_{y,C}|\psi_{\mathrm{int}}\rangle}},
\]
provided the denominator is nonzero. The resulting candidate weights are
\[
p_i' = \frac{|c_i|^2 L(y\mid x_i,C)}
{\sum_j |c_j|^2 L(y\mid x_j,C)}.
\]
Thus, contextual evidence reweights the candidate field, increasing support for compatible interpretations and reducing support for incompatible ones.

To represent the first commitment, the architecture selects $i^{\ast}=\arg\max_i p_i'$ for deterministic selection, or samples $i^{\ast}$ according to $p_i'$ when a stochastic policy is appropriate. The post-collapse state is
\[
|\psi_{\mathrm{int,coll}}\rangle = |x_{i^{\ast}}\rangle.
\]
Here, ``collapse'' or ``wave-packet collapse'' denotes this computational transition from a distribution over candidate interpretations to a selected state. It is not a claim about literal quantum measurement or physical wave functions in the brain. If the evidence is insufficient to support a stable commitment, the system may retain the updated distribution and request or await further evidence.

\subsection{Goal-Conditioned Action Superposition and Collapse}

After the interpretive collapse, the selected interpretation $x_{i^{\ast}}$ is passed to the goal module. Unlike the first stage, this second stage concerns which admissible action should be taken given the resolved interpretation, active goal, and regulatory constraints. Let $A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_k\}$ denote the quantized candidate action trajectories. Before commitment, the architecture represents them as a second weighted possibility field,
\[
|\psi_{\mathrm{act}}\rangle = \sum_{j=1}^{k} b_j |a_j\rangle,
\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{k}|b_j|^2=1,
\]
where $b_j$ encodes the current support for action $a_j$.

Let $G$ denote the active goal and $\mathcal{C}$ the admissibility constraints. An action-evaluation operator $N_{G,\mathcal{C},x^{\ast}}$ updates the action field according to goal relevance and admissibility. A diagonal form is
\[
N_{G,\mathcal{C},x^{\ast}} = \sum_j \sqrt{Q(a_j\mid x^{\ast},G,\mathcal{C})}\,|a_j\rangle\langle a_j|,
\]
where $Q(a_j\mid x^{\ast},G,\mathcal{C})\geq 0$ is a nonnegative compatibility score. The normalized post-update action weights are
\[
r_j = \frac{|b_j|^2 Q(a_j\mid x^{\ast},G,\mathcal{C})}
{\sum_\ell |b_\ell|^2 Q(a_\ell\mid x^{\ast},G,\mathcal{C})},
\]
provided the denominator is nonzero. The second commitment selects $j^{\ast}=\arg\max_j r_j$ or samples according to $r_j$, yielding
\[
|\psi_{\mathrm{act,coll}}\rangle = |a_{j^{\ast}}\rangle,
\qquad \pi^{\ast}=a_{j^{\ast}}.
\]
This is the second and distinct computational collapse. It is goal-conditioned action resolution, not semantic interpretation, and is not a claim about physical quantum measurement. If no action has sufficient support or admissibility, the system may defer action, seek further information, or retain the updated action distribution.

The architecture therefore has two sequential superposition-and-collapse stages: an evidence-conditioned collapse over interpretations and a goal- and constraint-conditioned collapse over actions. The two stages operate on different candidate sets and use different evidence. Language generation is not a third superposition; it realizes the selected interpretation-action pair.

\subsection{Knowledge Categories and Memory Storage}

The final component of the architecture concerns the relationship between abstract knowledge categories and the concrete memory substrate. In CCA, knowledge categories guide the interpretation of sensory evidence, while the memory field determines which candidate states remain viable under context and goal constraints.

Memory storage is therefore dual in nature: it contains explicit symbolic content and implicit geometric organization. Category labels shape the topology, while the memory field determines which candidate interpretations and action trajectories remain stable. This produces a memory system that is simultaneously structured and dynamically adaptive.

\subsection{Linguistic Response Module}

A central distinction of CCA is that it does not stop at internal interpretation. The architecture includes a Linguistic Response Module that converts the selected interpretation and action into natural language outputs that remain grounded in the resolved cognitive state and action path.

This design is essential for interactive AI. The system is not merely required to classify or retrieve information; it must also generate a response grounded in the same cognitive state that supports action selection. However, in CCA, the generation stage is not a further probabilistic superposition over candidate meanings. Once the interpretation and action states are stabilized, the response module converts those states into a natural language utterance.

The response module relies on the same five-stage linguistic analysis used for initial input analysis. The first stage estimates the agent and subject of the utterance; the second infers context and discourse constraints; the third identifies the communicative goal; the fourth constructs the semantic and pragmatic structure; and the fifth evaluates the final wording against the active task context. The result is a structured language plan that is consistent with the selected memory state.

This is not merely a final wording step. After an utterance is produced, it is re-embedded into the same cognitive loop as a candidate input and re-analyzed through the same five-stage schema. The system then compares the generated utterance against the original task goal, the intended summary, and the active memory state. If discourse alignment is insufficient, the architecture backtracks through the topological field and revises the output without discarding the selected interpretation.

This recursive procedure is essential because the architecture views generation as a loop rather than as a one-shot decoding process. The output is therefore not accepted solely because it is fluent; it must remain coherent with the original interpretation, task objective, and broader memory context.

\section{Mathematical Formulation}

\subsection{Categorical Consistency}

Let $\mathcal{K}$ denote the space of knowledge categories and $\mathcal{M}$ the space of memory states. Each concept $k \in \mathcal{K}$ is mapped to a memory representation $m \in \mathcal{M}$ through a category-conditioned embedding function
\[
F : \mathcal{K} \rightarrow \mathcal{M}.
\]
The mapping is assumed to preserve conceptual compatibility such that semantically related categories remain close in the memory manifold:
\[
d_{\mathcal{M}}(F(k_i), F(k_j)) \leq \epsilon \, d_{\mathcal{K}}(k_i, k_j),
\]
where $d_{\mathcal{K}}$ and $d_{\mathcal{M}}$ are appropriate distance functions over the category space and memory space, respectively. This condition ensures that structural similarity in the knowledge space induces a corresponding geometric organization in the memory field.

Let $\mathcal{E}$ denote the experience space and $\mathcal{K}$ the knowledge space. The active reasoning state is represented as a set of candidate memory vectors
\[
X = \{x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n\}, \quad x_i \in \mathbb{R}^d,
\]
where each $x_i$ is generated from the interaction between an experiential fragment $e_i \in \mathcal{E}$ and a categorical anchor $k_i \in \mathcal{K}$:
\[
x_i = g(e_i, k_i).
\]
Each $x_i$ corresponds to a memory unit, a concept, or a candidate perceptual state. The architecture therefore treats memory not as a flat lookup table, but as a structured topological manifold whose geometry reflects semantic compatibility and contextual relevance.

\subsection{Potential Energy and Relaxation Dynamics}

Reasoning is modeled as an energy minimization process over the candidate state space. We define a total potential energy
\[
E(X) =
\sum_{i=1}^{n} \alpha_i \|x_i - F(k_i)\|^2
+ \sum_{i<j} W_{ij}\,\psi(\|x_i - x_j\|)
+ \lambda \sum_{i=1}^{n} \rho(x_i),
\]
where:
- $\alpha_i$ weights the fidelity of each candidate state to its associated concept,
- $W_{ij}$ represents relational compatibility or historical association,
- $\psi(\cdot)$ is a distance-dependent interaction potential,
- $\rho(\cdot)$ penalizes unstable or inconsistent states,
- $\lambda$ controls the strength of regularization.

The reasoning dynamics follow a relaxation process:
\[
\dot{x}_i = -\eta \frac{\partial E(X)}{\partial x_i},
\]
where $\eta > 0$ is the relaxation rate. In this formulation, inference corresponds to movement through the energy landscape until a stable minimum is reached. Candidate states with low energy contribute stronger support to the candidate field and to subsequent evidence-conditioned interpretation.

\subsection{Phase-Based Contextual Coherence}

As an optional computational device, each memory element may be assigned a phase variable $\theta_i \in [0, 2\pi)$. The phase-coupled compatibility between two candidate states is given by
\[
\Phi_{ij} = \gamma_{ij}\cos(\theta_i - \theta_j),
\]
where $\gamma_{ij}$ measures the strength of contextual alignment between $x_i$ and $x_j$. A corresponding coherence energy is
\[
E_{\text{phase}}(X,\Theta)
= -\sum_{i<j} \gamma_{ij}\cos(\theta_i - \theta_j).
\]

The phase dynamics may be modeled by
\[
\dot{\theta}_i =
\sum_{j \neq i} \gamma_{ij}\sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) + \delta_i,
\]
where $\delta_i$ represents local contextual bias. Phase alignment is treated as an auxiliary coherence signal that can inform candidate compatibility or evidence weighting at either resolution stage. It is not interpreted as physical entanglement, and phase synchronization alone does not collapse either field; commitment occurs through the two evidence-conditioned update and selection rules defined above.

\subsection{Error Feedback and Topological Deformation}

Reasoning is not assumed to be error-free. Let the discrepancy between a predicted and observed state be
\[
e_i = \|x_i - x_i^{\ast}\|,
\]
where $x_i^{\ast}$ denotes the target or expected representation under contextual constraints. The architecture updates the memory topology through a deformation term
\[
\Delta T = \sum_i \beta_i e_i \nabla_{x_i} \mathcal{R}(x_i),
\]
where $\mathcal{R}$ is a correction operator and $\beta_i$ controls the intensity of adaptation. This mechanism allows the system to backtrack, reweight, or locally reshape the memory field when the current interpretation or action selection is inconsistent with the evidence.

\section{Reasoning \& Algorithms}

CCA does not model inference as a single symbolic production firing event. Instead, reasoning is formulated as an explicit search over a structured memory field in which perceptual candidates are evaluated under semantic compatibility, contextual evidence, and goal-specific constraints.

\subsection{Perceptual Grounding and Candidate Construction}

Let the current task context be represented by a set of sensory observations and memory fragments $C = \{c_1, \ldots, c_m\}$. Each fragment is first encoded as a particle-like representation and mapped into the current memory graph:
\[
 x_i \leftarrow \mathrm{Embed}(c_i, \mathcal{G}),
\]
where $\mathcal{G}$ denotes the current memory graph and its relational structure. Candidate states are then evaluated by a composite energy function
\[
E(X,\Theta) = E_{\mathrm{mem}}(X) + E_{\mathrm{rel}}(X) + E_{\mathrm{phase}}(X,\Theta) + E_{\mathrm{reg}}(X),
\]
where $E_{\mathrm{mem}}$ penalizes mismatch with category-conditioned targets, $E_{\mathrm{rel}}$ captures relational compatibility among neighboring states, $E_{\mathrm{phase}}$ is an optional auxiliary coherence term, and $E_{\mathrm{reg}}$ enforces regulatory constraints.

\subsection{Interpretive Superposition and Evidence-Conditioned Resolution}

Inference first updates candidate positions and, when used, auxiliary phase alignment:
\[
\dot{x}_i = -\eta \frac{\partial E(X,\Theta)}{\partial x_i},
\qquad
\dot{\theta}_i =
\sum_{j \neq i} \gamma_{ij}\sin(\theta_j - \theta_i) + \delta_i.
\]
After quantization, alternative interpretations are represented as a normalized candidate field $|\psi_{\mathrm{int}}\rangle=\sum_i c_i|x_i\rangle$. Contextual evidence $y$ updates its weights according to $p_i' \propto |c_i|^2 L(y\mid x_i,C)$, with normalization across candidates. The system then commits to a selected interpretation, for example by choosing the maximum-weight candidate or by sampling from the updated distribution. This first evidence-conditioned commitment is called interpretive collapse; it is a formal selection/update rule and not a claim of physical quantum measurement. If evidence remains insufficient, the updated distribution can be retained rather than forcing a premature commitment.

\subsection{Goal-Conditioned Action Superposition and Match \& Select}

After interpretive collapse, the selected state $x^{\ast}$ conditions a second weighted field over candidate actions. For $A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_k\}$, let $|\psi_{\mathrm{act}}\rangle=\sum_j b_j|a_j\rangle$ denote this action-level superposition. The active goal and admissibility constraints update the action weights according to $r_j \propto |b_j|^2 Q(a_j\mid x^{\ast},G,\mathcal{C})$. Normalizing the weights and selecting the maximum or sampling from them produces a second, goal-conditioned computational collapse to an action $a^{\ast}$. This second resolution is distinct from interpretive collapse: it answers what action to take, not what the evidence means. The terms superposition and collapse here denote a weighted candidate representation and its computational resolution, not physical quantum dynamics.

Formally, let $A = \{a_1, \ldots, a_k\}$ be the set of candidate actions and let $x^{\ast}$ be the selected interpretation. The goal-conditioned action score is
\[
\mathcal{Q}(a_j) = \mathcal{Q}(a_j \mid x^{\ast}, G, \mathcal{C}).
\]
The deterministic Match \& Select rule is then
\[
\pi^{\ast} = \arg\max_{a_j \in A} \; \mathcal{Q}(a_j \mid x^{\ast}, G, \mathcal{C}),
\]
which is equivalent to choosing the maximum-weight action when the action-field prior is uniform and the weights are proportional to the action scores. More generally, the prior action weights $|b_j|^2$ are combined with these scores before normalization. If no action is sufficiently supported or admissible, the system may defer commitment, seek additional information, or retain the updated action distribution.

\subsection{Linguistic Response Generation}

Once the action state stabilizes, the selected interpretation-action pair is transferred to a Linguistic Response Module. This module converts the selected states into a language response that is consistent with both the memory state and the task objective. The response process is recursive and self-monitoring, but it does not create a third superposed semantic stage. Instead, generation is a realization of already selected cognitive and action states.

The response process begins by parsing the task instruction or question into communicative intent, then extracts the core semantic content that the system intends to convey. The selected content is then mapped onto a linguistic frame, revised for discourse relevance, and checked against the original instruction or question. If a mismatch is detected, the system re-enters the topological memory field, backtracks to the relevant candidate set, and modifies the response accordingly.

\subsection{Loop Stabilization and Potential Convergence}

Inference in CCA proceeds in iterative cycles. At each cycle, the architecture performs the following sequence:

1. Encode the task context and active sensory fragments.
2. Construct or update the topological memory graph.
3. Quantize candidate states into representational units.
4. Initialize phase variables if the auxiliary coherence mechanism is used.
5. Compute the total energy and update candidate positions and optional phase-based coherence.
6. Form or update the interpretive candidate distribution using experience, knowledge, and contextual evidence.
7. Apply the evidence-conditioned update and commit to an interpretation when the evidence is sufficient; otherwise retain the updated distribution.
8. Construct or update a second candidate distribution over actions conditioned on the selected interpretation.
9. Apply goal- and constraint-conditioned action updating and commit to an action when support is sufficient; otherwise retain the action distribution or seek further information.
10. Convert the selected interpretation-action pair into a natural language utterance using the Linguistic Response Module.
11. Re-evaluate the generated utterance by feeding it back into the same analytic loop and revise it if discourse alignment is insufficient.

The continuous relaxation component can be expressed as
\[
X^{(t+1)} = X^{(t)} - \eta \nabla_X E(X^{(t)},\Theta^{(t)}),
\]
\[
\Theta^{(t+1)} = \Theta^{(t)} + \tau \,\mathcal{S}(\Theta^{(t)},\Gamma),
\]
where $\mathcal{S}$ denotes the optional phase-based coherence update. The interpretive evidence update and collapse follow the candidate-relaxation steps; the goal-conditioned action update and collapse follow interpretation selection. These are two distinct resolution operations over different candidate sets. The process terminates when the candidate distributions and selected interpretation-action pair stabilize, the energy decrease falls below a threshold, or additional evidence is required.

The key property is that the architecture is not a strictly feedforward pipeline. The reasoning loop is bidirectional: the system can move from observation to candidate interpretation, from interpretation to goal-conditioned action selection, and then from action to generated utterance, while retaining the ability to backtrack when the generated output fails to satisfy the original task objective.

\subsection{Comparison with Classical Production Rule Matching}

Classical ACT-R-like architectures treat reasoning as a largely implicit symbolic operation: working memory is compared against production conditions, and a single production is selected according to activation and utility. In CCA, this process is described in two candidate-resolution stages. First, the architecture maintains a weighted field over candidate interpretations and updates it with contextual evidence. Second, it maintains a weighted field over candidate actions and updates it with goal relevance and admissibility constraints. Each field is resolved separately, and language generation follows the selected interpretation-action pair.

The first difference is therefore sensory grounding. CCA does not assume that symbolic rules are applied to already-structured memory; it assumes that perception itself is the starting point of interpretation. The second difference is that both interpretation and action resolution are explicitly conditioned on their relevant evidence: context for interpretation and task goals and constraints for action. Consequently, CCA reframes cognition as a distributed process of perceptual grounding, topological inference, two-stage candidate resolution, and generative response refinement rather than as a sequence of discrete production firing events.

\section{Implementation \& Complexity}

CCA can be implemented as a hybrid symbolic-graph system in which the control layer maintains explicit task symbols and category labels while the topological layer maintains a weighted memory graph. The architecture is therefore compatible with graph neural or graph-based symbolic systems, while still retaining a goal-conditioned strategic layer.

\subsection{Mapping Architecture to Graph-Based Code}

Let each memory item be represented as a node $v_i$ with an associated latent state $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ and category label $k_i \in \mathcal{K}$. The relational structure is encoded in an adjacency matrix whose entries reflect semantic compatibility, retrieval history, and contextual similarity.

A minimal implementation can be organized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Node features: symbolic content, category assignment, activation value, and latent embedding.
    \item Edge weights: semantic compatibility, co-occurrence frequency, or learned affinity.
    \item State update: iterative gradient descent over the total energy $E(X,\Theta)$.
    \item Optional coherence update: phase-based compatibility adjustment over neighboring nodes.
    \item Interpretation selection: evidence-conditioned update of the interpretive candidate distribution followed by commitment when confidence is sufficient.
    \item Goal-conditioned action selection: construct and update a second action candidate distribution, then commit under task and regulatory constraints.
    \item Response: generation of natural language from the stabilized interpretation-action pair using the five-stage schema, followed by post-output adequacy checking and revision.
\end{itemize}

This design admits a direct computational interpretation. The topological field is a weighted graph $G=(V,E)$ with $|V|=n$ active memory elements and $|E|=m$ relations. Each node stores a latent representation, category label, and activation state, while edges encode compatibility and contextual affinity. Inference and selection then proceed over this graph using relaxation dynamics, optional coherence adjustment, evidence-conditioned interpretive resolution, and goal-conditioned action resolution.

\subsection{Expected Computational Complexity}

Let $n$ denote the number of active candidate states, $m$ the number of relational edges in the active memory graph, $d$ the dimensionality of each latent embedding, and $T$ the number of relaxation steps. For sparse memory graphs, the dominant cost of the update step is approximately
\[
O(n^2)
\]
for densely connected structures and
\[
O(m)
\]
when the graph is sparse and only local neighborhoods are instantiated. Each relaxation step requires evaluating the energy and its gradient, dominated by pairwise compatibility terms and optional coherence terms. The gradient computation therefore costs approximately
\[
O(m + n d),
\]
while for dense structures it becomes
\[
O(n^2 d).
\]
The optional phase-based coherence update similarly costs
\[
O(m)
\]
per iteration. Updating and normalizing the evidence-weighted interpretation distribution costs $O(n)$ once candidate likelihoods are available. Updating and normalizing the action distribution costs $O(k)$ for $k$ candidate actions. Therefore the total complexity over $T$ relaxation iterations is approximately
\[
O\bigl(T(m + n d) + n + k\bigr)
\]
for sparse implementations and
\[
O\bigl(T n^2 d + n + k\bigr)
\]
in the fully connected case. Memory usage is linear in the size of the graph and latent representation, with an additional $O(k)$ storage for action candidates:
\[
O(n d + m + k).
\]

This analysis shows that CCA is computationally feasible in sparse or locally connected regimes, while dense topological fields can become expensive as the number of candidate states grows. The two decision stages remain tractable as long as the interpretation and action candidate sets remain moderate relative to the graph size.

\subsection{Future Directions}

A natural extension is the development of a hybrid symbolic-neural implementation in which the topological layer is learned from data while the control hub retains explicit symbolic constraints. In such a system, action-level Match \& Select can be implemented through structured decision networks, while the memory field continues to model relational coherence and semantic ambiguity.

\section{Experiments / Evaluation}

To evaluate the behavioral advantages of CCA, we designed a set of controlled multimodal memory tasks that isolate the effects of topological relaxation, contextual evidence updates, and goal-conditioned action selection.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We constructed a synthetic memory graph with $n$ concept nodes, each assigned a latent embedding $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ and a category label $k_i \in \mathcal{K}$. Semantic relations were encoded in a weighted adjacency matrix, and each task specified a set of sensory fragments together with a target objective. We then compared CCA against two baselines:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a classical production-rule system with symbolic Match \& Select;
    \item a similarity-based retrieval model that ranks candidates by embedding distance alone.
\end{itemize}

For each task, we measured:
\begin{itemize}
    \item retrieval accuracy;
    \item convergence time, defined as the number of relaxation steps until the energy curve stabilized;
    \item concentration and stability of the interpretive and action candidate distributions after evidence updates;
    \item robustness under noise, defined as accuracy degradation under increasing corruption or distractor density;
    \item action-selection accuracy, defined as the probability of choosing the goal-compatible action under ambiguity;
    \item response quality under conversational settings, measured by semantic consistency between the inferred state and the generated output;
    \item discourse alignment, defined as the degree to which the generated response matches the original question, task objective, and intended summary.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Retrieval Under Semantic Ambiguity}

The first evaluation examines whether the architecture can resolve ambiguous memory states in which multiple candidate interpretations share similar symbolic or perceptual features. In these tasks, the system must distinguish semantically related but contextually irrelevant states before selecting a response or action.

CCA achieved substantially higher retrieval accuracy than the baseline models, particularly when distractors were conceptually close but contextually irrelevant. This improvement arises because candidate interpretations are retained until contextual evidence is evaluated; the evidence update increases the relative support of compatible candidates and suppresses contextually irrelevant ones before the first collapse and subsequent action selection.

\subsection{Ablation Study}

To test the contribution of each mechanism, we evaluated several ablated variants:
\begin{itemize}
    \item without phase-based coherence adjustment;
    \item without evidence-conditioned interpretive updating and collapse;
    \item without goal-conditioned action updating and collapse;
    \item without error-driven topological deformation;
    \item without category-conditioned mapping $F(\cdot)$;
    \item without the Linguistic Response Module;
    \item without the post-output adequacy check and revision loop.
\end{itemize}

The full model consistently outperformed all ablations. Removing phase-based coherence adjustment reduced retrieval robustness in the presence of competing candidates, while removing the interpretive evidence update reduced the system's ability to resolve context-sensitive ambiguity. Removing the action-level update led to poorer goal-compatible action choices. Removing the response module degraded language grounding and discourse consistency, while removing the revision loop reduced alignment with the original task objective.

\subsection{Convergence and Stability}

A second set of experiments measured convergence behavior over repeated retrieval cycles. We tracked the decay of the total energy $E(X,\Theta)$, the concentration and stability of the interpretive and action candidate distributions, and the stability of the selected interpretation-action pair over repeated iterations.

The full architecture converged reliably in tasks involving moderate semantic ambiguity, while the ablated variants exhibited slower stabilization or more frequent oscillation between competing candidate states. These results suggest that topological relaxation, contextual evidence updates, and goal-constrained action resolution jointly support robust inference.

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

Overall, the evaluation demonstrates that CCA improves retrieval, action selection, and response generation in semantically complex environments by explicitly modeling memory as a structured, dynamic field rather than as a flat symbolic store. The two-stage superposition-and-collapse design separates evidence-conditioned resolution of interpretations from goal-conditioned resolution of actions, allowing ambiguity to be represented before commitment at both stages.

\section{Discussion}

CCA should be interpreted as a non-linear reformulation of cognitive control rather than as a wholesale replacement for symbolic architectures. The central contribution is not that symbolic structure is removed, but that symbolic structure is embedded within a richer topological and evidence-sensitive reasoning process. This enables the architecture to represent ambiguity explicitly while still maintaining task-driven control.

This shift has several conceptual advantages. First, it makes memory retrieval explainable in geometric terms: candidate states are evaluated according to their position, compatibility, and coherence. Second, it distinguishes between interpretive ambiguity and action-level ambiguity, which are often conflated in standard production systems. Third, it integrates language generation with the same memory and control loop that supports action selection, thereby reducing the gap between internal deliberation and external communication.

At the same time, the framework should be understood as a theoretical abstraction rather than a literal physical theory of quantum cognition. The possibility-based candidate representations and wave-packet-collapse terminology are computational analogies for retaining alternatives, updating them with evidence or goals, and committing to selected candidates at two distinct stages. Phase variables, when used, provide an auxiliary coherence signal. None of these terms asserts literal wave functions, quantum measurement, or entanglement in the brain.

The limitations of the current formulation are also important. The model is presented at the conceptual and mathematical level, but it does not yet provide large-scale empirical validation across benchmark datasets or direct comparison against modern graph-based reasoning systems. Future work will need to establish whether the two-stage candidate-update and collapse model remains stable under larger and noisier task settings.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper introduced the Conscious Cognitive Architecture (CCA), a multimodal non-linear cognitive architecture that reformulates reasoning as a sensory-to-topological-to-interpretive-resolution-to-action-resolution-to-linguistic process. Rather than treating cognition as a single symbolic Match \& Select cycle, CCA separates interpretation from action selection and models both as sequential candidate-resolution stages: it maintains a weighted field over quantized candidate interpretations, updates and collapses that field using contextual evidence, then maintains a second weighted field over actions and collapses it using task goals and admissibility constraints.

The key contribution is therefore conceptual as well as formal: memory is treated not as a flat symbolic store but as a structured, relational field in which sensory evidence, category organization, and task constraints jointly determine the most coherent interpretation and action. The architecture distinguishes semantic uncertainty from goal-sensitive action selection. Its wave-packet-collapse terminology describes computational evidence-conditioned commitment at both stages, not physical quantum processes. Language generation is treated as a grounded realization of the stabilized interpretation-action pair rather than as an independent or additional superposed inference stage.

Future work will focus on empirical evaluation, sparse graph implementations, and hybrid symbolic-neural extensions that preserve interpretability while improving scalability. By grounding reasoning in a topological memory field and separating interpretive uncertainty from goal-conditioned action choice, CCA provides a coherent alternative to purely discrete production architectures.
